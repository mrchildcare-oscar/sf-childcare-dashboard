Notes to start a conversation

What could universal child care be?

In August, a room full of providers, parents, union members and organizers wrote values on sticky notes. The question was simple. If you could build the child care system from scratch, what would it stand on?

Nobody wrote "more paperwork." Nobody wrote "a new rating scale." People wrote dignity. People first. Language justice. For all.

"Universal child care" is coming up everywhere now. Politicians say it. Campaigns say it. It sounds like one thing. It is really many choices, and each choice decides who it works for.

These notes walk through the shape of that August conversation. They are meant to spark ideas, not settle them.

1. Where the current system comes from

The group started with history. The system we have grew from old ideas about whose work counts and which families deserve help. Care work done mostly by women, many of them immigrants and women of color, was treated as something less than real work.

Those roots touch everyone. Providers kept working through COVID and were among the last offered vaccines. Families learned to wait on lists for years and to expect less.

Any universal plan can be read with one question in mind: does it change those roots, or just add money on top of them?

2. This moment

Child care is winning. Voters in San Francisco, Alameda and Sonoma passed local child care measures by wide margins. New Mexico made child care free for most families after a long campaign. California will have a new governor soon.

When an issue starts winning, many people want to shape it. One participant put it plainly: some of the people coming for universal child care want a small version that is easy to win. A small version can leave people out.

3. Why we haven't won yet

  • Who was at the table. Transitional kindergarten became universal for four-year-olds. Family child care was not part of how it was built.
  • Scarcity that divides. One year the money goes to more slots. The next year it goes to wages. Families' needs and providers' needs get set against each other.
  • "Universal" in name only. Care gets split into buckets, each funded and ruled differently. Families and providers have to stitch the buckets back together.
  • Measuring what is easy to count. Ratings and reports stand in for the real work: one person caring for several children, many hours a day, in a home.
One union voice said it simply: we can raise wages all we want, but if children aren't coming through the door, it doesn't matter. A plan that only works for providers, or only for families, doesn't work.

4. The values people named

  • People first, people over profit.
  • Dignity and respect for providers and for families.
  • For all, with no income test to get in.
  • Providers and parents need each other, so both belong at the table.
  • Experience is quality. A degree does not define the worth of the work.
  • Real choice, not only for families who can pay.
  • Language justice and anti-racism. People who can't understand each other can't build together.
  • Flexible, living systems, not rigid funding streams.
  • Cultural dignity. Your culture is as precious as mine.
  • Providers' say over their own programs. Family child care providers are educators and small business owners.
  • All the children are our responsibility.

Some of these values center providers. Many center families, children and whole communities. Universal child care probably holds only if all of them are in it together.

5. Defining it ourselves

The organizers in August didn't hand the room a definition. They asked: we're saying universal child care, but what does it mean to us?

One way to start is to turn each value into a question we can ask of any proposal.

ValueQuestion to ask any proposal
For allWho qualifies? Is there an income limit, an age limit, or a residency rule? Who ends up outside?
Providers and parents togetherDoes the money follow the child to the family's choice of care, including a family child care home?
Experience is qualityWhat degrees or credentials will be required? Is there a path for providers already doing this work?
Real choiceDoes it cover early mornings, evenings, weekends? Does it include family, friend and neighbor care?
Flexible systemsIs it one simple stream of funding, or more separate buckets with separate rules?
DignityAre providers paid by enrollment or by attendance? Are benefits and time off included?
Language justiceIs language access built in from the start, or added later?
Free or partly freeIs care actually free, or is it a credit that leaves families paying part of the bill?

Some things to think about together

  1. If universal child care passed tomorrow, what is the one detail you would check first?
  2. What would make it work for families and providers at the same time?
  3. What happened with transitional kindergarten that we would not want to repeat?
  4. Who is not in our conversations yet, and should be?
  5. What is one value you would add to the list?

In August, someone said we can only get what we're organized to take.

Maybe the first step is just agreeing on what we mean by the words. Then we can recognize it when someone offers us a version of it.
